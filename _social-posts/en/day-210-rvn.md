  ### RVN: The buildup – warnings, outages and central dependency
  
  It feels like a storm. FAA ground stops, Microsoft cloud problems, banks stalling, reports about NSANet, supermarkets whose refrigerators go down. Officially it is almost always a bug, maintenance or overload. No coordinated attack. Yet the density stands out, especially when you look back at what was said earlier.
  
  ### What was already there
  
  In 2020, at the opening of Cyber Polygon, Klaus Schwab of the World Economic Forum sketched the “frightening scenario” of a comprehensive cyberattack that would bring the power supply, transportation, hospital services and “our society as a whole” to a complete halt. The COVID crisis would look like “a small disturbance” by comparison. In 2021 he repeated that a lack of cybersecurity had become “a clear and immediate danger.”
  
  In July 2024 a single faulty CrowdStrike update did what many of those warnings only described. Roughly 8.5 million Windows systems went down. Aviation, banks, hospitals and media worldwide were brought to a standstill. Officially not an attack, but a pure supply-chain cascade through one vendor. It was the largest IT outage in history up to that point.
  
  In 2025 ProPublica revealed that Microsoft had used Chinese engineers to maintain Pentagon cloud systems through a “digital escort” program. Defense Secretary Pete Hegseth called it a “breach of trust,” shut the program down and started an investigation into whether code had been added that the department did not know about.
  
  In October and November 2026 Bill Gates said in multiple interviews (including CBS) that we will probably have to experience “a few of the negatives” before the full political response arrives. He spoke of “a lot of negative surprises” in the cyber domain in the coming year, because “bad people” are using AI models without safeguards. A visible attack that causes “a lot of problems” would make people say the warners had been right.
  
  ### What is happening now
  
  In recent weeks outages have been stacking up at the FAA, Microsoft, banks (including Chase/Zelle), Apple services and reports about NSANet. There has also been a confirmed supply-chain attack via Optimizely (178 sites affected). In parallel Operation Tornado is running, the FAA’s new SMART system is being rolled out (with repeated funding requests) and ARTEX is warning about AI-driven penetration tools. Most of the recent incidents receive the official label “technical,” “maintenance” or “not an attack.”
  
  What does stand is the chronology of warnings plus incidents that make the dependency on central systems visible. One vendor, one update, one third party or one bug can hit entire layers – from air traffic and banks to cloud, Apple services and even reports about intelligence networks. Whether the current density is coincidence plus technical debt, opportunistic actors, or a catalyst effect as Gates describes, cannot be hard-proven or disproven with the available public records.
  
  ### OIM way out
  
  This is exactly why central gatekeepers and single points of failure are a risk.  
  Do not adopt one framing.  
  Do not treat the other framing as the end.
  
  Instead: what exactly is in the official statements.  
  Which supply-chain paths were actually in use.  
  Which warnings were made on the record and in what context.  
  What has and has not been investigated.
  
  Decentralization, self-management and cryptographic verification are not an abstract ideal here. They are the concrete counterpart to systems in which one update or one third party is enough to shut down critical layers.
  
  **Question for you**
  
  How many warnings and visible outages do you need before dependency on a single vendor no longer feels self-evident?
