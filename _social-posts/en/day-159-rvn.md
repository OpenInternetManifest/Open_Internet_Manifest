  ### RVN: Ceuta – the flame is there, the news barely
  
  At the end of July an estimated 70,000 to 80,000 people swam and climbed from Morocco into the Spanish enclave of Ceuta. A city of just over 80,000 residents.  
  Dozens to around a hundred people died in the crossing.
  
  Most went back within two days.  
  That became the end of the story.
  
  It was the end of many news bulletins.  
  Not of the city.
  
  ### What is happening now
  
  Weeks later thousands of people are still in Ceuta.  
  Madrid puts the figure at about 5,000.  
  The city itself and local estimates go to 10,000 or more.  
  Camps on the beach, in parks, by the hospital.
  
  In recent days it boiled over.  
  Residents drove people off Trampolín beach, set belongings on fire, blocked the Red Cross.  
  Migrants stoned military patrols.  
  Police fired into the air while clearing a camp.
  
  On Wednesday, Ceuta Day, an estimated 20,000 people marched in the enclave itself.  
  In Madrid tens of thousands more.
  
  ### Who did go
  
  Part of the independent press got on a plane after the headlines had already faded.
  
  Dutch Travel Maniac (Tom) walked Ceuta in early August, spoke to residents and migrants, and questioned the low official leftover figures.
  
  Nick Shirley filmed camps, the border and the mood in the city. He was chased and taken to his car by police.
  
  Rebel News sent Efrain Flores Monsanto and Alexa Lavoie.  
  Jon Clarke of The Olive Press walked the enclave and, from what he saw, put the remaining number around 10,000.
  
  Boots on the ground is no guarantee of a clean frame.  
  It is still something other than a desk in Hilversum.
  
  ### The gap
  
  A spike of 70,000 is news.  
  An aftermath of thousands in a far corner is management.  
  Management does not make the front page until the street forces it there.
  
  In the Netherlands it stayed largely quiet.  
  As if a European land border with Africa were a bit of foreign weather.

  How many came in.  
  How many went back.  
  How many remain.  
  Who counts, who films, who stays away.
  
  As long as the story is “solved” and the city shows something else, it is the same break: narrative first, verification later.

### RVN: Ceuta – the flame is there, the news barely

At the end of July an estimated 70,000 to 80,000 people swam and climbed from Morocco into the Spanish enclave of Ceuta. A city of just over 80,000 residents.  
Dozens to around a hundred people died in the crossing.

Most went back within two days.  
That became the end of the story.

It was the end of many news bulletins.  
Not of the city.

### What is happening now

Weeks later thousands of people are still in Ceuta.  
Madrid puts the figure at about 5,000.  
The city itself and local estimates go to 10,000 or more.  
Camps on the beach, in parks, by the hospital.

In recent days it boiled over.  
Residents drove people off Trampolín beach, set belongings on fire, blocked the Red Cross.  
Migrants stoned military patrols.  
Police fired into the air while clearing a camp.

On Wednesday, Ceuta Day, an estimated 20,000 people marched in the enclave itself.  
In Madrid tens of thousands more.

### Who did go

Part of the independent press got on a plane after the headlines had already faded.

Dutch Travel Maniac (Tom) walked Ceuta in early August, spoke to residents and migrants, and questioned the low official leftover figures.

Nick Shirley filmed camps, the border and the mood in the city. He was chased and taken to his car by police.

Rebel News sent Efrain Flores Monsanto and Alexa Lavoie.  
Jon Clarke of The Olive Press walked the enclave and, from what he saw, put the remaining number around 10,000.

Boots on the ground is no guarantee of a clean frame.  
It is still something other than a desk in Hilversum.

### The gap

A spike of 70,000 is news.  
An aftermath of thousands in a far corner is management.  
Management does not make the front page until the street forces it there.

In the Netherlands it stayed largely quiet.  
As if a European land border with Africa were a bit of foreign weather.

### OIM way out

Not a choice between hunting people and opening everything.  
The sequence.

How many came in.  
How many went back.  
How many remain.  
Who counts, who films, who stays away.

As long as the story is “solved” and the city shows something else, it is the same break: narrative first, verification later.

**Question for you**

Did Ceuta stay on your radar after the first week?  
And where did your picture come from: the news desk, or someone who was standing there?
