  ### RVN: MC-21 – The Lesson of Strategic Reversal
  
  In the West, almost no one knows what an MC-21 is.  
  Even among aviation enthusiasts it is barely known.
  
  Yet this aircraft is one of the clearest examples of a mechanism we far too rarely name.
  
  **What happened**
  
  After the invasion of Ukraine in 2022, the West imposed heavy sanctions on Russian aviation.  
  Boeing and Airbus stopped deliveries and maintenance. Leasing companies tried to reclaim their aircraft.
  
  Russia responded differently than expected:
  
  - A law made it possible to place foreign-leased aircraft on the Russian registry.
  - The vast majority of Boeing and Airbus planes remained in Russia.
  - Part of that fleet was kept flying by cannibalizing parts from grounded aircraft.
  
  At the same time, the development of the domestic MC-21 was rapidly Russified.  
  What had been a delayed project became a strategic priority.
  
  **The result**
  
  - Western leasing companies took billions in losses.
  - Boeing and Airbus lost an entire market as well as future maintenance revenue.
  - Russia bought time and accelerated the construction of its own competitor to the 737 MAX and A320neo.
  
  The side that thought it was punishing the other took a financial hit itself.  
  The sanctioned side used exactly the other party’s assets as a bridge toward greater independence.
  
  **The real lesson**
  
  This story is not primarily about Russia, about Boeing, or about one type of aircraft.  
  It is about a pattern:
  
  When you are dependent, you can be punished.  
  When you are forced to do it yourself, something sometimes emerges that would otherwise not have appeared — or only much later.
  
  The roles were reversed.  
  The side that thought it set the rules saw its own capital and technology used as a bridge to greater autonomy on the other side.
  
  This is not a moral judgment.  
  This is strategic observation.
  
  **OIM way forward**
  
  The question is not whether sanctions “work” or “do not work”.  
  The question is whether we are willing to see what they actually set in motion — including the unintended competitors and the accelerated self-reliance that can result from them.
  
  Doing it yourself is not always the easiest path.  
  Sometimes it is the only path left.  
  And sometimes that forced path turns out stronger than the dependent route one wanted to preserve.
  
  **Question to you**
  
  How often do we look only at the intention of a measure…  
  and how often at the mechanism it sets in motion?
